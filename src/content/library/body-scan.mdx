Most of the time, you notice your body only when it demands attention: a headache, a stiff neck, a stomach that won't settle. The body scan turns that around. You move your attention slowly from your feet to the top of your head, noticing whatever sensations are there, without trying to change them.

## Where the body scan comes from

The body scan became widely known through Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction, the eight-week programme Jon Kabat-Zinn developed at the University of Massachusetts Medical School in 1979. Adapted from older contemplative practices, it now appears in many mindfulness programmes.

Research on mindfulness programmes suggests modest benefits for stress and anxiety for many people. The body scan on its own has been studied less, and the results are more mixed. So treat it as an experiment: try it a few times and see what it does for you.

## Why noticing, not fixing, is the point

When something feels uncomfortable, the usual instinct is to get rid of it: stretch the tight shoulder, distract yourself from the churning stomach. That's often sensible. The body scan practises a different response: noticing a sensation, describing it plainly to yourself, and moving on.

Many people find that sensations become easier to be with once they stop fighting them. Tightness you're bracing against often feels worse than tightness you're simply observing. The scan also builds a habit of checking in, so you're more likely to catch early signals like a clenched jaw or held breath. And since feelings so often show up in the body first, it gives you raw material for understanding your emotions.

Noticing nothing is also a valid result. Some areas will feel neutral or hard to sense at all, and "I can't feel much in my knees" is a perfectly good observation.

## How to do it

1. Get comfortable, lying down or sitting. Close your eyes or let your gaze rest on the floor. If closing your eyes feels uneasy, keep them open.
2. Take a few natural breaths. There's no need to change them.
3. Bring your attention to your feet: toes, soles, heels. What's there? Warmth, coolness, the pressure of the floor, tingling, or not much at all.
4. Move slowly upwards through your ankles, calves, knees, thighs, and hips, spending a few breaths in each area.
5. Continue through your belly, lower back, chest, upper back, hands, arms, shoulders, and neck.
6. Finish with your face and head: jaw, mouth, eyes, forehead, scalp.
7. Before you finish, notice your body as a whole for a few breaths. Then open your eyes, look around, and move your fingers and toes.

A full scan can take 20 minutes or more, but a three-minute version works too. For the shortest version, check just your feet, belly, and jaw.

### When your mind wanders

It will. When you realise you've drifted, notice where your attention went, then return to the last part of the body you remember. Coming back is the practice, not a sign you're doing it wrong.

If you find pain, you're allowed to shift position. Noticing without fixing is about curiosity, not endurance.

<Callout title="If the body scan brings up distress">
For some people, especially those who have lived through trauma, turning attention inward can bring up panic, painful memories, or a sense of numbness. If focusing on the body brings up distress, open your eyes, look around the room, and stop. Naming a few things you can see, or pressing your feet into the floor, can help you come back to the present. Next time you might try a shorter scan, keep your eyes open, or stay with areas that feel neutral, like your hands. The body scan is not a substitute for professional support, and a trauma-informed therapist can help you work with your body at a pace that feels safe.
</Callout>

## Bringing it into your day

A quick scan helps you answer "where do I feel this?" with something more specific than "everywhere", which makes it a good warm-up for a [WellNest check-in](/check-in). Once you've found where a feeling lives, [giving it a colour, shape, and texture](/library/felt-sense) is a natural next step.

If looking inward tends to make things more intense, an outward-focused practice like [5-4-3-2-1 grounding](/library/grounding-5-4-3-2-1) may suit you better, at least for now.
