Some days a small setback rolls right off you. On other days the same thing tips you into panic or anger, or into a flat, faraway numbness. The window of tolerance is a simple way to make sense of that difference, and your body is often the first place you'll notice which side of it you're on.

## What the window is

The psychiatrist Dan Siegel introduced the term in his 1999 book *The Developing Mind*. It describes a zone of arousal, meaning how activated your nervous system is, in which you can handle what's happening. Inside your window you can feel emotions, even strong ones, and still think clearly, stay connected to the people around you, and respond rather than just react.

The width of the window isn't fixed. It tends to narrow when you're short on sleep, unwell, hungry, or under long-term stress, and it can be narrower for people who have been through trauma. It tends to widen with rest, support, and safety. Therapists who work with the body, including Pat Ogden and colleagues, have built the idea into their approaches to trauma therapy.

## Above and below the window

When stress pushes you past the edges of your window, it usually goes one of two ways.

### Hyperarousal: too much

This is the revved-up state. In your body you might notice a racing heart, fast or shallow breathing, a tight chest or jaw, heat, sweating, or restlessness. In your mind it can feel like racing thoughts, irritability, panic, or an urge to fight or get away. It becomes hard to listen, and small things feel huge.

### Hypoarousal: too little

This is the shut-down state. Your body might feel heavy, slumped, cold, numb, or exhausted, and moving takes effort. Your mind might feel foggy, flat, or disconnected, as if you're watching life from behind glass. It can be hard to speak or to care about things that usually matter to you.

Many people swing between the two, sometimes within the same day. Neither state is a character flaw. Both can be understood as your nervous system trying to protect you, just in ways that don't always fit the situation.

## Why your body is a useful early warning

Thoughts are good at explaining a state after the fact. "Of course I'm angry, look what they did." Your body often shows the shift earlier. A clenched jaw can show up before you've registered that you're irritated, and a heavy, sinking feeling can arrive before you realise you've checked out. A regular habit of checking in with your body makes those signals easier to catch.

A quick way to check where you are:

1. Notice your breath. Is it fast or slow, shallow or deep?
2. Notice your muscles. Are they braced and tight, or slack and heavy?
3. Notice your energy. Is it buzzy and restless, or flat and sinking?
4. Ask yourself: am I inside my window, above it, or below it?
5. Choose one small step that fits the answer.

## Coming back into the window

The steps that help depend on which way you've gone.

**If you're above the window**, try things that slow you down. Long, slow exhales like the [physiological sigh](/library/physiological-sigh) can help, as can [5-4-3-2-1 grounding](/library/grounding-5-4-3-2-1), pressing your feet into the floor, or splashing cool water on your face.

**If you're below the window**, try things that gently wake your system up. Stand up, stretch, or walk around. Look around the room and name what you see. Put on music with some energy, or reach out to someone you trust.

Aim for a little closer to the window, not perfect calm. Many therapists describe the longer-term goal as gradually widening the window, so that more of life fits inside it. Small, repeated moments of noticing where you are and finding your way back are part of how that happens. A [WellNest check-in](/check-in) is one place to practise.

<Callout title="When to reach out">
These ideas can help you understand yourself, but they're not a substitute for professional support. If you often find yourself far outside your window, or stuck there for long stretches, especially if it seems connected to past trauma, please consider talking with a mental health professional.
</Callout>
