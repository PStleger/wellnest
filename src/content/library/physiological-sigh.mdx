You already know how to do this one. Your body sighs on its own every few minutes, usually without you noticing. The physiological sigh borrows that built-in pattern on purpose: a double inhale through the nose, followed by a long, slow exhale. Repeated for a few minutes, it's often called cyclic sighing.

## What a sigh actually does

A spontaneous sigh is a deeper-than-usual breath, often with a second small intake stacked on top of the first. Humans and many other mammals do it automatically. One function physiologists broadly agree on is mechanical: over time, some of the tiny air sacs in your lungs, the alveoli, partially collapse, and a big sighing breath helps reopen them. In 2016, researchers working with mice identified a circuit in the brainstem that controls sighing, a reminder of how deeply wired the reflex is.

Sighing also seems to track emotional state. People tend to sigh more during stress and during relief, and some researchers have proposed that a sigh works as a kind of reset for a breathing pattern that has become tight or irregular.

## Why the long exhale matters

Your heart rate naturally slows a little on each out-breath and speeds up a little on each in-breath. When your exhale is noticeably longer than your inhale, you spend more of each breath in that slowing phase. The double inhale gives you a full lungful for an unhurried breath out.

The best-known evidence comes from a 2023 randomised study at Stanford. Just over a hundred adults practised one of four techniques for five minutes a day for a month: cyclic sighing, box breathing, a faster breathing technique with breath holds, or mindfulness meditation. People in every group reported feeling less anxious after their daily practice. Cyclic sighing produced the largest gains in positive mood, and a bigger drop in resting breathing rate than meditation did.

You may have seen the sigh described as the fastest way to calm down. That's a stronger claim than the research supports. The results are encouraging, but they come largely from one study, so it's best treated as a promising, low-risk tool worth trying for yourself.

## How to do it

1. Breathe in through your nose until your lungs feel comfortably full.
2. Without breathing out, take a second, shorter sip of air through your nose to top up your lungs. It's normal for this second inhale to be much smaller than the first.
3. Breathe out slowly through your mouth, letting the exhale run longer than both inhales together. Soft, slightly parted lips can help you slow it down.
4. Pause briefly if you like, then repeat.

If breathing out through your mouth feels awkward, you can do the whole thing through your nose. The [WellNest breathing exercise](/breathe) can pace you through it.

### One sigh or five minutes

In the moment, say after an email that made your stomach drop, one to three sighs may be enough to take the edge off. As a daily practice, the Stanford study used about five minutes of slow, repeated sighing. Try both.

<Callout title="Keep it slow and comfortable">
The goal is a full, relaxed breath, not a forceful one. If you feel dizzy or light-headed, you're probably breathing too hard or too fast, so pause and let your breathing return to normal. If you have a heart or lung condition, or if paying close attention to your breath tends to make you more anxious, go gently and check with a clinician if you're unsure.
</Callout>

## Where it fits

The sigh and [box breathing](/library/box-breathing) complement each other. Box breathing gives your mind a structure to hold on to, which can help when your thoughts are racing. The sigh needs no counting at all, so it's easy to use mid-conversation or when you're too flustered to keep track of numbers.

Neither is meant to switch off a feeling. A few slow sighs can bring your body down a notch so you can think more clearly about what you need. If you find you're relying on breathing exercises to get through most days, that's a good reason to talk with a doctor or therapist. These techniques can support that kind of help, but they don't replace it.
