When your mind is spinning, the room you're actually sitting in can feel far away. The 5-4-3-2-1 exercise brings you back to it by working through your senses one at a time: five things you can see, four you can feel, three you can hear, two you can smell, and one you can taste. It takes a minute or two, needs no equipment, and works with your eyes open in almost any setting.

## What grounding means

Grounding is a family of techniques that shift your attention away from an inner storm of worries, memories, or racing thoughts and towards the present moment, using your senses and your surroundings. Therapists often teach grounding to people dealing with anxiety, panic, or the aftermath of trauma. It appears, for example, in Seeking Safety, a widely used treatment programme for trauma and substance use, and dialectical behaviour therapy teaches a related set of skills for soothing yourself through the five senses.

The 5-4-3-2-1 version is one of the most widely shared, but its exact origins are unclear, and it has received little formal research on its own. Its popularity rests mostly on clinical experience and on how easy it is to remember. That's a reason to hold it lightly, not to dismiss it. Try it and see whether it helps you.

## Why the senses help

- **Your senses only work in the present.** Worry lives in the future and rumination in the past, but what you can see and hear right now is always happening now.
- **It gives your mind a specific job.** Searching for and counting things takes up mental bandwidth, which leaves less room for the thoughts that were spiralling.
- **It points your attention outwards.** When looking inward makes things feel bigger, attending to the room around you can be a relief.
- **It's concrete.** You're not trying to think your way out of a feeling. You're just noticing a lamp, a hum, the texture of your sleeve.

## How to do it

1. Pause and take one slow breath.
2. **Name five things you can see.** Say them silently or out loud. Be specific: not "a wall" but "a crack in the paint by the window".
3. **Name four things you can feel.** Your feet on the floor, the weight of your body in the chair, the fabric of your sleeve, air on your skin.
4. **Name three things you can hear.** Traffic, the hum of a fridge, your own breathing.
5. **Name two things you can smell.** If nothing is obvious, move to find something, like coffee, soap, or your own sleeve, or name two smells you like.
6. **Name one thing you can taste.** Toothpaste, the last thing you drank, or just the taste in your mouth right now.
7. Take another slow breath and notice whether anything has shifted, even slightly.

### Making it your own

- If you're very distressed, use smaller numbers. Three things you see, two you hear, one you feel is plenty.
- If a sense isn't available to you, skip it or swap in another.
- Add detail to slow yourself down. Name the colour, the shape, the texture.
- Say things out loud if you can. Hearing your own voice can help.
- Press your feet firmly into the floor as you go.

## When to reach for it

Grounding can help when you feel anxious or panicky, when you're caught up in a memory, after a nightmare, or when you feel spaced out and foggy. It can help on both sides of the [window of tolerance](/library/window-of-tolerance): calming things down when you're flooded, and gently bringing you back when you feel shut down or far away.

It's also a good fallback when inward practices like the [body scan](/library/body-scan) feel like too much. After a round of 5-4-3-2-1, a [WellNest check-in](/check-in) can help you notice what's going on underneath.

<Callout title="Grounding is a first step, not a treatment">
Grounding can help you steady yourself in the moment, but it isn't a substitute for professional support. If you often feel disconnected from your surroundings, find yourself caught in flashbacks, or feel overwhelmed much of the time, please talk with a doctor or mental health professional. They can help you understand what's happening and find support that fits.
</Callout>
