Box breathing is about as simple as a breathing exercise gets: breathe in for four counts, hold for four, breathe out for four, hold for four, and repeat. The even rhythm gives a racing mind something steady to follow, and the slow pace tends to settle your body along with it. Nobody around you has to know you're doing it.

## What box breathing is

Picture a square. Each side is one phase of the breath, and each lasts the same number of counts: in, hold, out, hold. At four seconds per side, that's fewer than four breaths a minute, well below the 12 to 20 typical for an adult at rest.

Versions of the technique are taught in some high-stress professions, including military and police training, as a way to stay clear-headed under pressure. It can be just as useful before a difficult conversation, or at 3 a.m. when your mind won't switch off.

## Why a slow, even rhythm can help

Your heart rate naturally rises a little as you breathe in and falls a little as you breathe out. Slow, full breathing tends to make those swings larger and more regular. A 2018 review of 15 studies in healthy adults found that slow breathing was associated with higher heart rate variability, often treated as a marker of a flexible, well-regulated nervous system, and with people reporting more relaxation and less anxiety. That's a small evidence base, so it's best read as promising rather than conclusive.

The counting matters too. Holding a number in mind gives an anxious brain a small, manageable job, which can crowd out some of the spiralling.

In a 2023 study, adults practised box breathing or one of three other techniques for five minutes a day over a month. People in every group, including a mindfulness meditation group, reported feeling less anxious after their daily session. An exhale-focused technique, the [physiological sigh](/library/physiological-sigh), did best on some measures. The takeaway: box breathing is a reasonable, low-cost tool, not a cure-all.

## How to do it

1. Sit or stand in a way that feels steady. If it helps, rest one hand on your belly.
2. Breathe out gently until your lungs feel comfortably empty.
3. Breathe in through your nose for a count of four.
4. Hold your breath for four. Keep your throat, jaw, and shoulders soft rather than clamping down.
5. Breathe out slowly for four.
6. Hold, empty, for four.
7. Repeat for four to six rounds. Then let your breathing return to its own rhythm and notice how you feel.

The [WellNest breathing exercise](/breathe) paces each side for you, so you can follow along instead of counting.

### Adapting the counts

There's nothing special about four. What matters is an even, unhurried rhythm you can keep up comfortably.

- **If four feels too long**, try three counts per side, or even two.
- **If the holds feel uncomfortable**, shorten them: in for four, hold for two, out for four, hold for two. Or drop the holds and simply breathe in and out for equal counts.
- **If you notice air hunger**, that gasping urge to breathe, your counts are too long. It should feel steady, not strained.

<Callout title="Go gently with breath holds">
Stop and breathe normally if you feel dizzy, light-headed, tingly, or panicky. For some people, focusing on the breath raises anxiety rather than easing it. If you have a heart or lung condition, are pregnant, or aren't sure breath holds are right for you, skip the holds or check with a clinician first. Never practise breath holds in water.
</Callout>

## When to reach for it

Box breathing tends to work best as a way to steady yourself in the moment: before a presentation or a hard phone call, when you notice your breathing has become fast and shallow, or when you're lying awake with your thoughts looping. Practising for a few minutes on calm days makes the rhythm familiar for when you need it most.

It isn't meant to make difficult feelings disappear, just to turn the volume down so you can decide what to do next. If anxiety is frequent or intense, breathing exercises can sit alongside professional support, but they are not a substitute for it.
