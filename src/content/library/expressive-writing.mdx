Some days leave a residue: the conversation you keep replaying, the worry that won't settle, the thing you couldn't say. Expressive writing is a simple, well-studied way to move some of that out of your head and onto the page, where you can look at it instead of circling around it.

## What the research says

The social psychologist James Pennebaker began studying this in the 1980s. In a 1986 study with Sandra Beall, university students wrote for about 15 minutes a day, four days in a row. Those who wrote about both the facts of a deeply upsetting experience and their feelings about it made fewer visits to the campus health centre over the following months than students who wrote about superficial topics, even though the writing left them more upset in the short term.

Hundreds of studies have followed. A 2006 meta-analysis of 146 randomised studies found a real but small average benefit, with results varying a lot between studies. So expressive writing is best seen as a low-cost tool that helps many people somewhat, not a guaranteed fix or a replacement for therapy.

## Why putting it on paper might help

Researchers still debate why it works when it does, but a few ideas stand out:

- **Words turn a swirl into a sequence.** Writing asks you to put looping, fragmented feelings into some kind of order.
- **You get a little distance.** Once something is on the page, you can read it instead of only being inside it.
- **You make meaning over time.** In Pennebaker's later analyses, people who seemed to benefit most used more words about causes and understanding as their sessions went on, like *because*, *realise*, and *understand*. They were building a story that made sense, not just venting.

If finding words for your feelings is hard, [name it to tame it](/library/name-it-to-tame-it) can help.

## How to do it

1. Find a private spot and set a timer for 15 to 20 minutes, or 10 if that's what you have.
2. Write about what's weighing on you: your deepest thoughts and feelings about it. Not only what happened, but how it affected you and how it connects to other parts of your life.
3. Keep writing until the timer ends. Don't worry about spelling or grammar. If you get stuck, repeat what you've already written.
4. Write only for yourself. You can delete it or throw it away afterwards.
5. When the timer ends, stop. Take a few slow breaths before moving on to something ordinary.
6. If it's useful, repeat on three or four days in a row, as in many of the original studies.

You can do this in the [WellNest journal](/journal/new).

### Prompts for after a hard day

- What happened today that I keep coming back to, and what does it stir up?
- What did I feel in the moment, and what do I feel about it now?
- If a friend had this day, what would I understand about how they reacted?
- What did I need today that I didn't get?
- What do I understand now that I didn't when I started writing?

## When it's too much

Feeling a bit stirred up afterwards is common, as it was in the original studies, and usually passes. Overwhelmed is different.

Pennebaker's own rule of thumb: if you feel that writing about something would push you over the edge, don't write about it right now. Choose something less loaded, or write about how today felt in your body instead. With very raw events, it can help to wait until you have a little distance.

<Callout title="Know when to stop">
If you start to feel flooded, panicky, or numb and far away, stop writing. Put the page aside, look around the room, feel your feet on the floor, and take a few slow breaths. You can come back another day, or not at all. Expressive writing is not a substitute for professional support, and if a particular experience keeps overwhelming you, it may be worth working through it with a therapist.
</Callout>

If writing often leaves you flooded or shut down, the [window of tolerance](/library/window-of-tolerance) can help you understand why.
