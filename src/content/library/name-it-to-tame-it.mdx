When a feeling hits hard, it can be a blur: something is wrong, and you want it to stop. Finding a word for it, like *frustrated*, *ashamed*, or *lonely*, sounds too simple to matter. But a line of research suggests that naming a feeling can take some of the heat out of it.

## What the research found

Psychologists call this **affect labelling**, and the psychiatrist Dan Siegel popularised a catchier name for it: "name it to tame it". One of the best-known studies was published in 2007 by Matthew Lieberman and colleagues at UCLA. Participants lay in a brain scanner and looked at photos of faces showing strong negative emotions. In some trials they picked which of two emotion words, such as "angry" or "scared", described the face. In others they did different tasks, like matching the face to another with the same expression.

Labelling the emotion was linked with lower activity in the amygdala, a region involved in detecting threat, and higher activity in a part of the prefrontal cortex associated with regulating emotional responses. The more one went up, the more the other tended to go down.

It's worth keeping this in proportion. It was a small lab study using photos of strangers' faces, not people's own experiences. Later research has found related effects, including lower reported distress in some studies, but the effects tend to be modest. Naming a feeling is a helpful everyday skill, not a switch that turns emotions off, and not a substitute for professional support when feelings regularly become unmanageable.

## Why more words help

The psychologist and neuroscientist Lisa Feldman Barrett uses the term **emotional granularity** for the ability to make fine distinctions between feelings. Someone with low granularity might describe most unpleasant states as "stressed" or "bad". Someone with higher granularity might tell apart disappointed, resentful, homesick, and humiliated.

Research suggests people who draw finer distinctions between their negative emotions tend to cope better with them, for example being less likely to drink heavily or lash out when distressed. Those findings are correlational, so they don't prove that learning more emotion words will change your behaviour. But there's a practical logic to it: different feelings point to different needs. *Lonely* might call for reaching out, *exhausted* for rest, and *resentful* for a boundary. "Bad" doesn't tell you much about what to do next.

Emotional vocabulary can grow. Here are some alternatives to common catch-all words:

- Instead of **stressed**: overwhelmed, pressured, scattered, on edge, stretched thin
- Instead of **sad**: disappointed, lonely, grieving, homesick, discouraged, hurt
- Instead of **angry**: irritated, resentful, betrayed, indignant, fed up
- Instead of **anxious**: uneasy, dreading, jittery, insecure, apprehensive
- Instead of **bad**: ashamed, guilty, embarrassed, jealous, regretful
- Instead of **fine**: content, relieved, calm, flat, numb, tired

## How to do it

1. Notice that something is happening. You might feel it in your body first.
2. Try a first word. A rough guess is fine.
3. Ask yourself "what else?" or "more precisely?" Angry might turn out to be embarrassed, and then angry about being embarrassed.
4. Say it plainly, silently, out loud, or in writing: "I'm feeling disappointed" or "there's some dread here".
5. Notice what happens next. Often the intensity eases a little. If not, that's fine too.
6. Ask what this feeling might need from you.

Mixed feelings are normal. You can be relieved and sad at once, and naming both is more accurate.

## Name it, don't narrate it

Labelling works best when it's brief. There's a difference between "I feel humiliated" and going over, again and again, why it happened and what it says about you. The second is closer to rumination, which tends to keep feelings going. If you catch yourself spiralling into explanation, come back to the word and to what you feel in your body. [Giving the feeling a colour, shape, and texture](/library/felt-sense) is one way to do that.

When you do want to explore the story, [expressive writing](/library/expressive-writing) gives it a container. For everyday practice, a [WellNest check-in](/check-in) is a good moment to find the word.
