When a feeling is big, it can seem to fill everything. You don't so much *have* anxiety as *become* anxious, all the way through. The core WellNest exercise offers a small shift: find where the feeling lives in your body, then describe it as if it were an object, with a colour, a shape, and a texture.

## Where the idea comes from

It draws on two traditions. The first is **Focusing**, a practice developed by the philosopher and psychologist Eugene Gendlin at the University of Chicago. Gendlin's research with colleagues suggested that therapy clients who made progress tended to pause and sense into something they could feel but couldn't yet put into words. He called that bodily, not-yet-clear sense of a situation a *felt sense*. In Focusing, you notice the felt sense, then look for a word, phrase, or image that fits it, checking back with your body to see if it does.

The second is **somatic tracking**, a technique from Pain Reprocessing Therapy, an approach to chronic pain developed by the psychotherapist Alan Gordon. Somatic tracking means watching a bodily sensation with curiosity and a sense of safety, rather than alarm, and noticing how it shifts from moment to moment. WellNest isn't a pain treatment, but it borrows that stance: interested, unhurried, not forcing anything.

## Why describing a sensation helps

This sounds playful, and it is. It also does some useful work.

- **It gives your attention somewhere to rest.** Vague distress is hard to stay with, so the mind bounces away into worrying. A heavy grey band across your chest is something you can actually stay with.
- **It creates a little distance.** There's a difference between "I'm a mess" and "there's a tight, fist-sized knot in my stomach". The second is something you're noticing, not everything you are.
- **It moves you from story to sensation.** The story about a feeling, why it happened and what it means, often keeps it going. Sensations belong to the present moment.
- **It lets you notice change.** Once you've described something, you can tell when the colour fades, the edges soften, or the weight shifts.

This particular exercise hasn't been tested on its own in clinical trials, but it's closely related to research on putting feelings into words, covered in [name it to tame it](/library/name-it-to-tame-it).

## How to do it

1. Pause and name the feeling, even roughly: "something like dread". It's fine if you can't.
2. Ask where you feel it: chest, throat, stomach, shoulders, face, hands. If nothing stands out, notice where you feel the most tension.
3. Give it a colour. Go with the first one that comes. There are no wrong answers.
4. Give it a shape and a size: a ball, a band, a cloud, something jagged, something as big as your fist.
5. Give it a texture: heavy, prickly, hot, hollow, sticky, buzzing, dense.
6. Stay with it for a few breaths, with curiosity rather than alarm. Don't try to change it. Just watch.
7. Check in again. Is it the same, softer, sharper, somewhere else?

## About the 90 seconds

The neuroanatomist Jill Bolte Taylor, writing about her recovery from a stroke in *My Stroke of Insight*, described the idea that the chemical surge of an emotion runs its course in about 90 seconds. If a feeling lasts much longer, she suggested, it's often because we keep restarting it with our thoughts.

It's best to treat this as a helpful heuristic, not a scientific law. Research on how long emotions last finds a wide range, from moments to days, depending on what's happening and how much we dwell on it. Still, the idea captures something many people recognise: feelings tend to come in waves, and staying with the sensation rather than feeding the story often lets a wave crest and ease. If it hasn't eased after a minute and a half, nothing has gone wrong.

<Callout title="If staying with it feels like too much">
You're always allowed to stop. If a sensation gets more intense or you start to feel overwhelmed, look around the room and bring your attention to something neutral, like your feet on the floor. This exercise is a way of getting to know your feelings, not a substitute for professional support.
</Callout>
